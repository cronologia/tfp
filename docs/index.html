TFP — Cronologia

Chronology of the Brazilian Society for the Defense of Tradition, Family and Property and of Plinio Corrêa de Oliveira (1908–present)

An open, source-referenced chronology of the TFP — Sociedade Brasileira de Defesa da Tradição, Família e Propriedade, founded in São Paulo on 26 July 1960 by the lay Catholic leader Plinio Corrêa de Oliveira — its campaigns, its international network, the 1985 CNBB note, the post-1995 succession dispute, and the two branches of today: the Heralds of the Gospel and the Instituto Plinio Corrêa de Oliveira.

About

Work-in-progress dataset on a religiously and politically contested subject. Four source families are labeled throughout: movement/sympathetic (IPCO, tfp.org, arautos.org), ecclesiastical (CNBB, Vatican), academic (Zanotto, Caldeira, CPDOC-FGV) and critical/press/ex-member accounts. Campaign numbers are the movement's own and are attributed; 'sect' accusations are presented as attributed accusations with the movement's replies; no accusation is asserted as fact. Corrections against primary sources are welcome.

What it is
A lay Catholic traditionalist civic movement founded in São Paulo in 1960 by Plinio Corrêa de Oliveira, known for street campaigns against communism and agrarian reform, and for a network of autonomous sister TFPs on four continents.[2] [29]
Founded
26 July 1960, São Paulo — consensus date across sources; co-founder lists vary slightly.[2] [15]
Doctrinal matrix
'Revolução e Contra-Revolução' (1959): Plinio's reading of five centuries of 'Revolution' (pseudo-Reformation → French Revolution → communism) and the 'Counter-Revolution' against it — his technical terms, always attributed to the text.[13] [1]
The 1985 CNBB note
In April 1985 a CNBB commission declared 'notorious the lack of communion of the TFP with the Church in Brazil', citing 'esoteric character, religious fanaticism, cult of the leader's personality' — the movement published replies; the note's full text circulates mainly via those replies.[21] [22]
The split
After Plinio's death (1995), the majority wing under João Scognamiglio Clá Dias founded the Heralds of the Gospel (1999, pontifical approval 2001) and won the courts' award of the TFP name (2003–04); the founders' wing created the Instituto Plinio Corrêa de Oliveira (2006).[30] [5] [6]
Today
Two branches claiming Plinio's legacy — the Heralds (under a pontifical commissioner since 2019) and the IPCO — plus an active international TFP network (American TFP, TFP France, Piotr Skarga in Poland, and others).[40] [34] [14]

1960 1960-11 Brazil 'Reforma Agrária: Questão de Consciência' — Four authors — Bishop Geraldo de Proença Sigaud, Bishop Antônio de Castro Mayer, Plinio Corrêa de Oliveira and the economist Luiz Mendonça de Freitas — publish the anti-agrarian-reform manifesto; about 30,000 copies over four editions (movement figures, attributed).[23] [19]
1963 1963 Rome The Coetus Internationalis Patrum — Sigaud, Castro Mayer and Marcel Lefebvre articulate the conservative minority of Vatican II; movement sources describe Plinio as the group's lay strategist — that role rests on sympathetic sources and is attributed.[7] [24]
1965 1960s ? Brazil The street campaigns — Caravans under red standards with a golden lion, public rosaries and signature drives against communism and 'socialist and confiscatory' agrarian reform make the TFP a fixture of Brazilian streets.[2] [20]
1968 1968-07/09 Brazil and Cone Sul The petition to Paul VI — The 'Message to Paul VI' against 'communist infiltration' in the Church gathers 1,600,368 signatures in Brazil (2,025,201 with the Southern Cone TFPs) — the movement's own numbers, always attributed.[20] [25]
1970s
1970 1970 Brazil Sigaud breaks with the TFP — Archbishop Sigaud breaks with Plinio and the movement, supporting the Médici government's agrarian program and diverging on liturgical-ecclesial questions.[7]
1973 1967–1983 ? Americas and Europe The international network — Sister TFPs form in Argentina, Chile and Uruguay (1967), then Spain (1971), the United States (incorporated 18 May 1973 as the Foundation for a Christian Civilization — PT Wikipedia says 1974; the divergence is recorded), Canada (1975), France (1977), Germany (1982) and beyond.[11] [2]
1980s
1984 1984 Venezuela The Venezuelan crisis — A press campaign and parliamentary inquiry target the 'Resistencia-TFP', with a member arrested on suspicion of a plot against John Paul II and accusations of being a 'sect'; the accusations never produced a conviction in the sources consulted, the Venezuelan TFP was effectively extinguished by around 1986, and every characterization here is attributed.[3] [43]
1985 1985-04 Brazil The CNBB note — A CNBB commission (Bishops Gilberto Pereira Lopes, Antonio Misiara, Carlos Alberto Navarro) declares 'notorious the lack of communion of the TFP with the Church in Brazil', citing 'esoteric character, religious fanaticism, cult of the leader's personality and abusive use of the name of Mary', and exhorts Catholics not to join. 1995 1995-10-03 São Paulo, Brazil Death of Plinio Corrêa de Oliveira — The founder dies at 86.[1]
1997 1997 São Paulo, Brazil The succession dispute begins — The majority wing led by João Scognamiglio Clá Dias sues over the statute reserving voting rights to the nine founding members; about 80% of the 1,800 members side with it.[30] [29]

2009 2005/2009 Rome The Heralds' clerical societies — The Heralds' societies Virgo Flos Carmeli (men) and Regina Virginum (women) are raised by Benedict XVI to societies of apostolic life; Clá Dias is ordained priest and made monsignor.[5]

Key figures

Plinio Corrêa de Oliveira

1908–1995 · Brazil

Lay founder and lifelong leader: constituent deputy 1933–34, Catholic Action president 1940–44, Legionário director, PUC-SP history professor, founder of Catolicismo (1951) and the TFP (1960), author of 'Revolução e Contra-Revolução' (1959) and 'Nobreza e Elites Tradicionais Análogas' (1993). Historians examine anti-Jewish passages in his 1930s articles in A Ordem (attributed); he called Vatican II 'one of the greatest calamities in the history of the Church' (1976).[1] [12]

Antônio de Castro Mayer

1904–1991 · Brazil

Bishop of Campos (coadjutor 1948, diocesan 1949, resigned 1981); patron of Catolicismo; co-author of 'Reforma Agrária: Questão de Consciência'; broke with Plinio and the TFP in the 1980s (details a documented gap); co-consecrator at Écône 1988 → declared excommunication; died April 1991 — sources diverge between 25 and 26 April. The bridge figure to the fsspx project, where his Campos succession is charted.[8] [48]

Geraldo de Proença Sigaud SVD

1909–1999 · Brazil

Bishop of Jacarezinho 1947–60, archbishop of Diamantina 1960–80; secretary of the Coetus Internationalis Patrum; co-author of 'Reforma Agrária: QC'; broke with the TFP in 1970 and did not follow Lefebvre.[7]

João Scognamiglio Clá Dias

b. 1939 · Brazil

Plinio's right hand in the TFP; leader of the majority wing after 1995; founder of the Heralds of the Gospel (1999); ordained priest (2005 per PT Wikipedia, ordination year flagged for verification) and made monsignor; resigned the generalate in June 2017 during the apostolic visitation; press accusations of abuse of authority and finances are attributed.[5] [37]

Disambiguation & nuance

The TFP sits at crowded intersections of Brazilian traditionalism; these cards keep the lines straight.

TFP ≠ SSPX

The TFP is a LAY movement founded and led by a layman; the SSPX is a priestly fraternity (Lefebvre). The TFP did not adhere to the 1988 consecrations and remained formally in communion. Castro Mayer is the biographical bridge between the two worlds — co-author with Plinio in the 1950s–60s, co-consecrator at Écône in 1988; his line is charted in the fsspx project.[8] [3]

TFP vs Heralds vs IPCO

The Heralds spring from the TFP's post-1995 majority wing (Clá Dias) and hold canonical status; the IPCO represents the founders' minority wing, without canonical statute, heir of the original style. The courts gave the TFP name to the Clá Dias wing; symbolically both claim Plinio.[30] [6]

'Counter-Revolution' is a technical term

In Plinio's 'Revolução e Contra-Revolução' (1959) the 'Revolution' is a five-century process (pseudo-Reformation → French Revolution → communism, later a 'fourth revolution' of culture) and the 'Counter-Revolution' its ordered reversal — definitions to be attributed to the text, not conflated with generic usage.[13]

Two 'TFPs' today

The Brazilian civil entity of that name (Heralds' wing) and the international istfp/IPCO network are distinct — beware of citing 'the TFP' in the present tense without specifying which.[40] [31]

Accusations are attributed

The 'sect'/'slavery' accusations (Venezuela 1984, the French 1995 report, press investigations of the Heralds) produced no judicial conviction in the sources consulted; this site records them as attributed accusations together with the movement's replies.[44] [45] [37]
